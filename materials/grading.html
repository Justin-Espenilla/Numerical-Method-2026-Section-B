How laboratory work is marked, what each rubric band means, and the rules governing the use of artificial-intelligence assistants. Read this before the first submission; it is not revised during the semester.

2. What every assignment is marked on

Point allocations differ between assignments, but each one distributes its marks across the same five categories. The proportions are approximately:

CategoryTypical shareWhat is being assessed
Correct implementation30 %The method is built as specified, runs, and produces the right numbers.
Verification25 %Order checks, comparison with exact or reference values, residuals, unit checks. An unverified correct answer scores in the same band as an unverified wrong one.
Engineering interpretation20 %What the number means for the structure, the channel, or the soil; significant figures; the recommendation.
Presentation10 %Labelled figures, captioned tables, units everywhere, a report that can be read in ten minutes.
AI Prompt Log15–30 %Marked on the rubric in section 5. Weighting rises through the semester.

3. Rubric bands

Every marked item falls into one of five bands. The descriptors below are applied to each part of an assignment separately, then summed.

BandShare of the part's pointsDescriptor
Exemplary90–100 % Method correct and verified by an independent check. Results interpreted in engineering terms with defensible significant figures. Limitations of the method identified without being asked. Could be handed to a project engineer unchanged.
Proficient75–89 % Method correct, verification present but partial or mechanical. Results interpreted, though the discussion restates rather than reasons. Minor presentation faults.
Developing60–74 % Method essentially correct with a definite error, or correct with no verification at all. Results reported without interpretation. Significant figures unjustified.
Beginning40–59 % The method is attempted and misunderstood in part. Numbers are produced but not checked and not believed. Figures unlabelled.
Insufficient0–39 % Not attempted, does not run, or the submitted work is not the student's own.
The single most consequential rule

A correct answer with no verification cannot exceed the Developing band. In practice an engineer is never asked "is your number right?" — they are asked "how do you know?" The verification is not an appendix to the work. It is the work.

4. AI policy

Artificial-intelligence assistants may be used for any part of any assignment in this course. This is deliberate: you will use them in practice, and learning to supervise them is part of your training.

Permitted, and expected

  • Explaining a method or an error message.
  • Drafting code you then verify and correct.
  • Checking your algebra or your derivation.
  • Improving the wording of your report.
  • Suggesting test cases.

Not permitted

  • Submitting AI output you have not verified.
  • Submitting code you cannot explain line by line.
  • Omitting prompts from the log, including the failed ones.
  • Reconstructing a log after the fact to fit the code.
  • Any use at all during the midterm examination.
Consequences

A missing AI Prompt Log is a zero for the assignment, not a deduction. A log that does not match the submitted work — prompts that could not have produced that code — is referred as academic dishonesty. Being unable to explain your own submitted code during a bench check reduces the assignment to the Insufficient band regardless of its content.

By contrast, a log that honestly records "I used the AI's whole function, here is how I tested it, here is the bug I found and fixed" is exactly what is being asked for and scores well.

4.1 Submitting the log

Complete the AI Prompt Log Template in Microsoft Word and submit it as lab<NN>_ai_prompts_<surname>.docx. Prompts must be reproduced word for word. Screenshots are not accepted; the text must be selectable so that it can be checked.

If you used no AI assistance, write "No AI assistance was used" in the first block and complete the required experiment stated in the assignment. The experiment is a graded exercise in prompt quality, not a confession.

5. The AI Prompt Log rubric

Each log is placed in one of four levels. The level sets the score for the whole Part D or E, scaled to its point value.

LevelScorePrompt qualityVerificationCritique
Level 4
Supervising		90–100 % Prompts state the engineering context, the constraints, the required interface and the prohibitions. Follow-up prompts narrow in on the weakness of the previous answer. Every AI-supplied result is tested against an independent value: an exact solution, a hand calculation, a unit check, or a convergence ratio. Identifies a real defect in a plausible-looking answer, quotes the offending line, and shows the correction. States where the AI was more useful than the student and where it was not.
Level 3
Directing		75–89 % Prompts specify the task and the main constraints, but leave assumptions to the model. Some iteration. Results are spot-checked, typically against one reference value. Notices that something was missing or wrong, but the diagnosis is general rather than specific.
Level 2
Requesting		55–74 % Single-line prompts of the form "write code for X". No context, no constraints. The code is run and, if it produces a number, accepted. Reports what the AI produced without evaluating it.
Level 1
Copying		0–54 % Prompts absent, paraphrased, or clearly reconstructed afterwards. None. None. Frequently the submitted code contains defects the student did not notice.
How to reach Level 4

The distinguishing feature is not the number of prompts, and not whether the AI was correct. It is whether the student found something. Every AI answer to an engineering problem contains at least one unstated assumption: equal spacing, a fixed term count, a missing pivot, an absent guard, a wrong wetted perimeter. A Level 4 log names that assumption, demonstrates it numerically, and corrects it.

5.1 Worked marking example

Log entryLevelWhy
"Prompt: write python code for simpsons rule. It gave me the code. It worked. I used it." 1 No verification, no critique, and the code almost certainly assumes equal spacing while the assignment data does not.
"Prompt: write a Simpson's rule function that accepts unequally spaced x and y arrays and falls back to the trapezoidal rule on the final strip if the number of intervals is odd. I tested it against ∫01exdx and got 1.718282 against 1.718282. But on my fuel data it returned 3,412 L against my own 3,398 L. The difference was that it applied the 1/3 rule to unequal strips, which is only second-order accurate there, not fourth. Line 14 assumes h = x[1]-x[0]. I replaced it with a segment-wise loop." 4 Specific prompt, verified against an exact integral, discrepancy investigated to the line, correction made and explained.

6.2 Seeded data

Several assignments generate data from a seed equal to the last two digits of your student number. State the seed in the report. Two students submitting identical seeded results is treated as collusion unless their student numbers end in the same two digits, in which case both must show independent working.

7. Bench checks

At any laboratory session you may be asked to explain a section of your own submitted code, or to make a small modification to it at the bench — for example, to change the stopping criterion or to add a guard. This takes about five minutes and forms part of the in-laboratory performance mark. It is the mechanism by which the disclosure policy is enforced, and it is far more informative than any similarity check.

8. Regrading

Requests for regrading are accepted within seven days of a mark being returned, in writing, and must identify the specific item disputed and the reason. The whole assignment is then re-marked, which may raise or lower the total. Requests of the form "I need a higher mark" are not considered.
