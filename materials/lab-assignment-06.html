Lab Assignment 6 · 100 points

Solving a Truss

Build a Gauss elimination solver with partial pivoting, prove that pivoting is not optional, assemble the equilibrium equations of a real truss, and verify the answer by structural equilibrium rather than by trusting the solver.

Part A — The solver 25 pts

  1. A.1 Implement gauss_naive(A, b) — elimination without pivoting — and gauss_pivot(A, b) — with partial pivoting and a singularity check. Neither may modify the arrays passed in; demonstrate that in your report.
  2. A.2 Implement residual(A, x, b) returning the maximum absolute residual, and use it after every solve in this assignment.
  3. A.3 Verify both routines on a 3×3 system whose solution you compute by hand. Show the hand solution.
  4. A.4 Count operations. Instrument the elimination loop with a multiplication counter and report the count for n = 5, 10, 20, 40. Compare with n3/3 and state the ratio.

Part B — Why pivoting is not optional 25 pts

  1. B.1 Solve the zero-pivot system
    [A] = [[0, 2, 1], [1, 3, 2], [2, 1, 4]],   {b} = {7, 13, 15}
    with both routines. Report what gauss_naive returns and why.
  2. B.2 Solve the near-singular pair 0.0003x1 + 3.0000x2 = 2.0001, x1 + x2 = 1 with both routines. The exact solution is (1/3, 2/3). Report the relative error of each, and the value of the elimination multiplier that caused the damage.
  3. B.3 Repeat B.2 with the coefficient 0.0003 replaced by 3×10−6, 3×10−9 and 3×10−12. Tabulate the relative error of the naive solver against the pivot size, and state the trend.
  4. B.4 For each case in B.3, report the residual of the naive answer. You will find cases where the residual is small and the answer is badly wrong. Explain, in three sentences, why a small residual is not proof of an accurate solution.

Part C — A real truss 30 pts

A simply supported pin-jointed truss spans 9 m in three 3 m panels. The lower chord joints are L0 (0, 0) with a pin support, L1 (3, 0), L2 (6, 0) and L3 (9, 0) with a roller support. The upper chord joints are U1 (3, 2.4) and U2 (6, 2.4).

The nine members are: L0L1, L1L2, L2L3 (bottom chord), U1U2 (top chord), and L0U1, U1L1, U1L2, U2L2, U2L3 (web). Vertical downward loads of 40 kN act at L1 and L2, and 20 kN at U1 and U2, giving 120 kN in total.

Check the determinacy before you start

m + r = 9 + 3 = 12 and 2j = 2(6) = 12, so the truss is statically determinate and the equilibrium equations alone are sufficient. Confirm this in your report. Had the count come out unequal, the method of joints would not have produced a square matrix — and your solver would have failed for a structural reason, not a numerical one.

  1. C.1 Draw the truss, label every joint and member, and state your sign convention (tension positive is recommended). This drawing is worth marks: an unlabelled truss cannot be checked.
  2. C.2 Assemble the equilibrium equations by the method of joints. Write the assembly as a loop over joints in your code, not as a hand-typed matrix — a hand-typed 12×12 matrix cannot be marked for method.
  3. C.3 Solve with gauss_pivot. Report every member force in kN to three significant figures, stating tension or compression for each.
  4. C.4 Verify in three independent ways, and report all three:
    1. the maximum residual of the linear system;
    2. ΣFx and ΣFy at every joint, computed from the member forces;
    3. global equilibrium: the two reactions must sum to the total applied load of 120 kN.
  5. C.5 Identify the most heavily loaded member in tension and in compression. For the compression member, state why its length matters to a designer even though this analysis never mentions buckling.
  6. C.6 Move the 40 kN load from L1 to L2, so that L2 carries 80 kN and L1 carries none, and re-solve. Tabulate both sets of member forces side by side. Report:
    1. any member that changes sign;
    2. any member that was zero-force and is now loaded, or the reverse;
    3. the member whose force increases the most, in kN and in percent.
    Then answer in one paragraph: a junior designer proposes deleting the zero-force members found in the first load case, on the grounds that they carry nothing. Using your two result sets, explain why that is unsafe.
A hint that saves an evening

Build and solve a three-member truss first, one you can check on paper in five minutes. Only when that is exactly right should you extend the assembly loop to the full truss. Students who assemble all twelve equations before testing anything spend the whole session hunting a sign error.

Required prompt experiment — the missing pivot

Send this prompt, word for word:

write python code for gauss elimination to solve Ax=b

Then, in the log:

  1. State whether the returned code performs partial pivoting. Quote the lines that do, or state that none do.
  2. Run its code on the zero-pivot system from B.1 and record exactly what happens.
  3. Run its code on the near-singular system from B.2 and report the relative error.
  4. Write an improved prompt that requires partial pivoting, a singularity check with a stated tolerance, non-destructive handling of the input arrays, and a returned residual. Compare the two answers.
  5. Identify one remaining defect in the improved answer. Common ones: the singularity tolerance is absolute rather than scaled to the matrix, the row swap copies the wrong slice, or the docstring claims something the code does not do.
