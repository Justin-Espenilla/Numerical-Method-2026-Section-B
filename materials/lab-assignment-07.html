Part A — Factor once, solve many 25 pts

  1. A.1 Implement lu_decompose(A) with partial pivoting and lu_solve(LU, piv, b). Verify that [L][U] reproduces the row-permuted [A] to within 10−12.
  2. A.2 A 60-degree-of-freedom frame is represented by a random symmetric matrix made diagonally dominant (build it as A = M + M.T + 60*np.eye(60) with M = rng.random((60,60)), seed = last two digits of your student number). Generate 40 load vectors. Solve all 40 cases twice: once by repeating full Gauss elimination, once by a single factorisation plus 40 triangular solves.
  3. A.3 Report the wall-clock time of both approaches and the speed-up factor. Compare with the theoretical prediction and explain any discrepancy.
  4. A.4 Confirm both approaches give the same answers to within 10−10, and report the maximum residual across all 40 cases.
  5. A.5 Plot the speed-up against the number of load cases from 1 to 40. State the number of load cases at which factorisation begins to pay, and explain why it is not 1.

Part B — Seepage under a sheet-pile wall 30 pts

Steady two-dimensional seepage is governed by Laplace's equation. On a uniform grid the finite-difference form is

hi,j = hi+1,j + hi−1,j + hi,j+1 + hi,j−14

Use a 40 × 25 grid representing a 20 m × 12.5 m soil block. Upstream water level 6.0 m of head on the left third of the top surface; downstream level 1.5 m on the right third; the impermeable sheet pile is a vertical no-flow boundary descending 5 m from the surface at mid-width; the base and sides are impermeable.

  1. B.1 State the boundary conditions you imposed and how a no-flow boundary is represented in the difference equation. Show the modified stencil.
  2. B.2 Solve by Gauss–Seidel to a tolerance of 10−6. Report the number of sweeps, the final maximum change, and the final maximum residual.
  3. B.3 Verify diagonal dominance for your assembled system before iterating, and report the result of the check.
  4. B.4 Sweep the relaxation factor λ from 1.00 to 1.95 in steps of 0.05. Plot iterations against λ and report the optimum and the speed-up it achieves over plain Gauss–Seidel.
  5. B.5 Plot the head field as a contour map with the sheet pile drawn on it. Identify the region of steepest hydraulic gradient and state its engineering significance for piping.
  6. B.6 Estimate the exit hydraulic gradient immediately downstream of the wall using your Lab 1 differentiator, and compare it with a critical gradient of 1.0. State whether the design is safe against piping and with what factor of safety.

Part C — Conditioning 25 pts

The Hilbert matrix, aij = 1/(i+j−1), arises whenever a polynomial is fitted by the normal equations. It is the standard example of an ill-conditioned system, and it is not artificial: it is what you get from an ordinary least-squares fit of a high-order polynomial.

  1. C.1 For n = 3 to 13, build the Hilbert matrix, choose the exact solution {x} = {1, 1, …, 1}, compute {b} = [A]{x}, then solve for {x} and measure the error.
  2. C.2 Tabulate n, condition number, maximum absolute error, and maximum residual. Plot the condition number and the error against n on a semilog axis.
  3. C.3 At which n does the answer lose all significance? Compare against the rule of thumb: figures lost ≈ log10(cond).
  4. C.4 Identify a case where the residual is at round-off level while the error is of order 1. Quote both numbers and explain, in four sentences, how both can be true simultaneously.
  5. C.5 State the practical consequence for a civil engineer fitting a high-order polynomial to survey or laboratory data, and name one thing you would do differently.
