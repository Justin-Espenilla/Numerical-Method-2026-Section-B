Lab Lecture 1 · Lecture topic: Physical Meaning of Derivatives

The Derivative You Can Actually Measure

In lecture, the derivative was a limit as h goes to zero. In the field, h is whatever your sensor's sampling interval happens to be, and it never goes to zero. This lab is about computing rates of change from a column of numbers — and about the fact that the sensor is lying to you a little, every single sample.

1. The story: nobody hands you f(x)

Case file — municipal water district, flood-control office

A reservoir level sensor records depth every 15 minutes. After a typhoon the district engineer is asked a simple-sounding question: at what moment was the reservoir filling fastest, and how fast?

She opens the file expecting an equation. There is no equation. There are 96 numbers per day, each with a timestamp, each rounded to the nearest centimetre by the logger.

The lecture taught her that the rate of filling is dh⁄dt. The lecture also assumed somebody would hand her h(t). Nobody did. Nobody ever does.

The central shift in this course

The derivative in your calculus class is an operation on a formula. The derivative in engineering practice is an estimate computed from samples. Same physical meaning — slope, rate, sensitivity — but a completely different computational problem, with an error term you are responsible for reporting.

2. From the limit to something you can compute

The definition from lecture:

f′(x) = limh→0 f(x+h) − f(x) h

Delete the limit. Keep h finite — say, the 900 seconds between samples. What you have left is a finite difference, and it comes in three flavours depending on which neighbours you are willing to use.

NameFormulaUsesErrorWhere you must use it
Forward [fi+1 − fi] / h the point and the one ahead O(h) the first data point — there is nothing behind it
Backward [fi − fi−1] / h the point and the one behind O(h) the last data point; also real-time systems, where "ahead" has not happened yet
Centred [fi+1 − fi−1] / 2h both neighbours, not the point itself O(h2) everywhere in the interior — it is free accuracy
Why centred is better, in one picture

Forward difference gives you the slope of the chord from here to the next point — that chord is systematically tilted in the direction the curve is bending. The centred difference uses a chord straddling the point, so the bending error on the left cancels the bending error on the right. Same number of data points, no extra cost, and the error drops from O(h) to O(h2). In Lab 09 you will derive exactly why, from the Taylor series.

3. Building the differentiator

Step 1 — the three formulas, verbatim

import numpy as np

def forward(y, h):
    """Forward difference, O(h). Length n-1."""
    return (y[1:] - y[:-1]) / h

def backward(y, h):
    """Backward difference, O(h). Same numbers as forward, shifted meaning."""
    return (y[1:] - y[:-1]) / h

def centred(y, h):
    """Centred difference, O(h**2). Valid at interior points only. Length n-2."""
    return (y[2:] - y[:-2]) / (2 * h)

Forward and backward compute the identical array — the difference is entirely in which x you attach the answer to. That bookkeeping is where most beginner bugs live, so we handle it explicitly:

Step 2 — a derivative for the whole series, endpoints included

def derivative(x, y):
    """dy/dx at every sample of an equally spaced series.

    Interior points  : centred,  O(h**2)
    First point      : forward,  O(h)
    Last point       : backward, O(h)

    Returns an array the same length as y.
    """
    x, y = np.asarray(x, float), np.asarray(y, float)
    h = x[1] - x[0]
    if not np.allclose(np.diff(x), h):
        raise ValueError("Samples are not equally spaced.")

    d = np.empty_like(y)
    d[1:-1] = (y[2:] - y[:-2]) / (2*h)   # interior
    d[0]     = (y[1]  - y[0])   / h           # first
    d[-1]    = (y[-1] - y[-2])  / h           # last
    return d
The endpoints are your weakest numbers

Notice that your first and last derivatives are one order less accurate than everything in between. On a reservoir record, those are precisely the points an engineer stares at — "how fast was it rising when the storm hit?" Report them with a caveat, or upgrade them with the three-point one-sided formula:

f′(x0) ≈ −3f0 + 4f1 − f2 2h   = O(h2)

Step 3 — check it against something you know

Never trust a differentiator you have not tested on a function whose derivative you can write down.

x = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 50)
y = np.sin(x)
d = derivative(x, y)
err = np.abs(d - np.cos(x))

print(f"max interior error: {err[1:-1].max():.3e}")
print(f"error at first point: {err[0]:.3e}")   # notably worse — as predicted

Then double the number of samples and watch the interior error drop by roughly four. That factor of four is your experimental proof that the centred formula really is second order. Getting a factor of two instead means you have a bug — almost always a wrong h.

4. The physical meanings, all at once

The lecture said the derivative means slope, rate, and sensitivity. The same three lines of code deliver all three; only the units change.

Rate

dh/dt from reservoir depth = inflow rate, m/s. Where it peaks is the moment of fastest filling — the answer the district engineer needed.

Velocity and acceleration

Differentiate position to get velocity; differentiate again for acceleration. Each pass costs you accuracy — you will see exactly how much below.

Sensitivity

∂output/∂input tells you which input deserves the expensive precision instrument. This is Lab 02, and it is the single most useful thing a derivative does in design work.

Direction of steepest change

Follow the negative derivative downhill and you have minimised something. That is Lab 03 — gradient descent — and it is the same formula you just wrote.

The second derivative

f″(xi) ≈ fi+1 − 2fi + fi−1 h2   = O(h2) 
def second_derivative(y, h):
    """Centred second difference at interior points. Length n-2."""
    return (y[2:] - 2*y[1:-1] + y[:-2]) / h**2

Look hard at that numerator. For smooth data the three terms nearly cancel — you are subtracting numbers that are almost equal — and then you divide by h2, which for a small h is a large multiplier applied to whatever survived. This is the amplification problem, and with real data it is not a subtlety. It is the whole story.

5. Where it breaks: noise is a derivative's natural enemy

Case file — back at the flood-control office

She runs the differentiator on the raw logger file and gets a plot that looks like a seismograph. Inflow apparently swings between +40 and −35 m³/s every fifteen minutes. The reservoir is not doing that. The reservoir is doing something smooth. The logger is doing that.

Reproduce her problem in four lines. The signal is smooth; the noise is one part in a thousand — far below what you would notice on a plot of the data itself:

rng   = np.random.default_rng(0)
x     = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 200)
clean = np.sin(x)
noisy = clean + rng.normal(0, 1e-3, x.size)   # 0.1 % noise — invisible

print(np.abs(derivative(x, clean) - np.cos(x)).max())   # ~1e-3, fine
print(np.abs(derivative(x, noisy) - np.cos(x)).max())   # ~0.2, a disaster
Why differentiation amplifies noise

The random error in each sample is about σ. The centred difference divides a difference of two such samples by 2h, so the noise in your derivative is roughly σ√2⁄(2h). With h = 0.03 and σ = 10−3, that is 0.024 — but the second derivative divides by h2 = 0.001, so its noise is hundreds of times the true signal.

The cruel part: sampling more finely makes h smaller, which makes truncation error better and noise amplification worse. Buying a faster logger can make your rate estimates less accurate. This is the same V-shaped trade-off you will formalise in Lab 09.

Two honest fixes

Fix 1 — smooth, then differentiate. A moving average removes noise but also flattens real peaks. The window length is a judgement call you must defend:

def moving_average(y, window=5):
    """Centred moving average. window must be odd; edges are handled by shrinking."""
    if window % 2 == 0:
        raise ValueError("window must be odd")
    kernel = np.ones(window) / window
    return np.convolve(y, kernel, mode='same')

smoothed = moving_average(noisy, 9)
print(np.abs(derivative(x, smoothed) - np.cos(x))[10:-10].max())   # ~0.01

Fix 2 — fit, then differentiate the fit. Fit a low-order polynomial over a small sliding window and take the derivative of the polynomial analytically. Noise gets averaged out by the least-squares fit instead of amplified. This is the Savitzky–Golay filter, and it is what commercial instruments do internally. You will meet the least-squares machinery behind it in Lab 07.

The engineering judgement

There is no "correct" amount of smoothing. Too little and you report noise as physics; too much and you erase the peak you were asked to find. What is not optional is stating what you did and showing the before-and-after. An unlabelled smoothed curve in a report is, quietly, a form of data fabrication.

7. What to carry into Lab 02

  • A derivative from data is an estimate with an order of accuracy you must state.
  • Centred beats one-sided by a whole order, for free — use one-sided only at the ends.
  • Differentiation amplifies noise; a second derivative amplifies it catastrophically.
  • Smaller h is not automatically better once real data is involved.
