Integrals That Earn Their Keep

"Area under the curve" is a picture, not a purpose. In practice the integral is how you get discharge from a river survey, work from a force record, the true heating power of a distorted waveform, and the point where a shape balances. Same code as Lab 04 — but now you have to decide what to integrate, and that is the part that is actually hard.

1. The story: the river that would not be measured

Case file — hydrology survey, tributary gauging station

A survey team needs the discharge of a river: cubic metres per second. You cannot measure that directly. There is no discharge meter.

What they can measure is depth at intervals across the channel, and flow velocity at each of those points. They come back with two columns of twelve numbers. The chief asks for one number by Friday.

The move that makes it an integral

Take a vertical sliver of the cross-section, width dx. Its area is H(x) dx, and the water in it is moving at U(x). So that sliver carries U(x)H(x) dx cubic metres per second. Add up all the slivers:

Q = ∫0B U(x) H(x) dx   [m³/s]

Notice what happened. The thing you integrate is not one of your measured columns — it is their product. This is the step students skip, and it is the entire difficulty of applied integration: translating a physical question into an integrand.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([0, 1.2, 2.4, 3.6, 4.8, 6.0, 7.2, 8.4, 9.6, 10.8, 12.0])   # m from bank
H = np.array([0, 0.4, 0.9, 1.5, 1.9, 2.1, 2.0, 1.6, 1.1, 0.5, 0.0])       # depth, m
U = np.array([0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.1, 1.2, 1.15, 0.95, 0.7, 0.35, 0.0])     # velocity, m/s

def trapz_data(x, y):
    dx = np.diff(x)
    return np.sum(dx * (y[:-1] + y[1:]) / 2)

A = trapz_data(x, H)          # cross-sectional area, m^2
Q = trapz_data(x, U * H)      # discharge, m^3/s   <-- the product
print(f"area  = {A:.3f} m^2")
print(f"Q     = {Q:.3f} m^3/s")
print(f"U_avg = {Q/A:.3f} m/s")
The mistake that gets marked wrong every year

Computing the mean velocity as U.mean() and multiplying by the area. That averages velocity over distance across the channel, giving equal weight to the shallow edges and the deep middle. The physically correct mean velocity is discharge-weighted: Q/A. Compare the two numbers in the code above — they differ by around 15 % on this cross-section, always in the same direction. Ask yourself which way, and why.

2. The pattern behind every application

Once you see the structure, every integral application in engineering is the same three-step move:

  1. Slice. Cut the problem into pieces so small that the quantity of interest is constant within a piece.
  2. Write the contribution of one slice. This is the integrand, and this is where the physics lives.
  3. Sum, then take the limit. That is the integral — and numerically, that is Lab 04's code.

Units are your correctness check

Before writing a single line of code, multiply the units of your integrand by the units of your integration variable. If the result is not the unit of the answer you want, your integrand is wrong and no amount of Simpson's rule will save you. This check costs ten seconds and catches most application errors.

3. Four applications worth knowing

3.1 Work from a real force record

A tensile test machine logs force against extension. The area under that curve is the energy absorbed by the specimen — and the point where the curve turns over is where it started to fail.

def work_done(x, F):
    """Work = integral of F dx. x in metres, F in newtons, result in joules."""
    return trapz_data(x, F)

Trivial code, non-trivial interpretation: if the force record includes an unloading segment where F is still positive but x is decreasing, your np.diff goes negative and the integral correctly subtracts that energy back out. The mathematics handles signed areas without being told. Most students expect a bug there and instead find physics.

3.2 RMS — the average that actually matters for power

A distorted AC waveform does not heat a resistor according to its mean value. It heats according to the root mean square:

Vrms = √ 1T ∫0T v(t)2 dt 
def rms(t, v):
    """Root-mean-square of a sampled waveform over its full record."""
    T = t[-1] - t[0]
    return np.sqrt(trapz_data(t, v**2) / T)

Three lines, and every one of them is a decision. We integrate v2, not v — because power goes as the square. We divide by the period — that is the "mean." We take the square root last — that is the "root." For a pure sine of amplitude A you must recover A/√2 = 0.7071A; check that before trusting it on anything distorted.

Why the mean is the wrong average here

The mean of a full sine cycle is exactly zero, yet a sine wave certainly heats a resistor. Averaging the square removes the sign, and the square is what appears in P = v2/R. Choosing which quantity to average is a physical decision, not a mathematical one — and it is the same decision you made when you weighted river velocity by depth.

3.3 Centroid — where the shape balances

x̄ = ∫ x H(x) dx ∫ H(x) dx first moment / area 
def centroid_x(x, H):
    """Horizontal centroid of the area under H(x)."""
    return trapz_data(x, x * H) / trapz_data(x, H)

The same code computes the centre of mass of a bar with varying density, the centre of pressure on a submerged gate, and the mean of a probability distribution. They are one formula wearing three uniforms: a weighted average, where the weight is whatever physically accumulates.

3.4 The average value of a function

f̄ = 1b−a ∫ab f(x) dx

This is the number to report when someone asks for "the average temperature during the test" and your samples are unevenly spaced in time. A plain mean() of unevenly spaced samples over-weights whichever period you happened to sample densely. The integral version weights by duration, which is what "average over time" means.

4. Where it breaks

Integrating the wrong thing

By far the most common failure, and no numerical check will catch it. Your discharge code will happily return a number for ∫U dx. It is just not discharge. Do the units check.

Unweighted averages

mean() on unevenly spaced or physically weighted data is silently wrong. If the samples are not equally spaced, or the quantity accumulates with a weight, you need the integral.

Sparse sampling near the peak

Twelve points across a river is a coarse grid, and the deep fast middle contributes most of Q. Error concentrates where the integrand is largest. Sample densely where it matters, not uniformly.

Squaring amplifies noise

RMS integrates v2, so a noise spike contributes its square. A rare glitch that barely shifts the mean can visibly inflate the RMS. Inspect the record before trusting the number.

5. Estimating your error without knowing the answer

Field data has no exact value to compare against. Use the order of the method instead. Integrate with n strips and with n/2 strips; for a second-order rule, Richardson extrapolation says the error in the finer estimate is about

E ≈ In − In/23   ⇒   Ibetter ≈ In + In − In/23 
def richardson_trapz(x, y):
    """Estimate the error of the full-resolution trapezoid using every other point.

    Requires an odd number of samples (so the coarse grid is exact halves).
    Returns (I_fine, I_coarse, error_estimate, I_improved).
    """
    I_fine   = trapz_data(x, y)
    I_coarse = trapz_data(x[::2], y[::2])
    err      = (I_fine - I_coarse) / 3
    return I_fine, I_coarse, err, I_fine + err

This gives you an error bar to put on the discharge report — from the data you already have, at the cost of one extra summation. It is the single most professional thing you can add to an applied integration result, and almost nobody does it.
