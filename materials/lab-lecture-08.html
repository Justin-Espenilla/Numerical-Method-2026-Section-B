Lab Lecture 8 · Lecture topic: Infinite Series

Infinite Series and the Cost of Stopping

The classical solution for consolidation settlement is an infinite sum. So is the deflection of a plate, the temperature in a curing concrete pour, and the response of a beam to a moving load. A computer cannot add infinitely many terms. This lab is about deciding where to stop, and about the arithmetic that decides the answer for you if you are careless.

1. The story: settlement, term by term

Case file — embankment on soft clay, settlement prediction

A geotechnical engineer must state when 90 % of the primary consolidation of a 6 m clay layer will be complete. Terzaghi's one-dimensional theory gives the answer as a series: the average degree of consolidation U at time factor T is

U(T) = 1 − ∑m=0∞ 2M2 e−M2T     M = π(2m+1)2

The textbook prints a table of U against T and stops there. The engineer needs values between the tabulated points, so the series must actually be summed — and summing it requires a decision no textbook makes: how many terms.

2. What convergence actually costs

Each term of the consolidation series decays as e−M2T, and M grows linearly with m, so the exponent grows as m2. For a large time factor the series collapses almost immediately. For a small one it does not.

The general lesson

Convergence rate is not a property of the series alone; it is a property of the series at the value you are evaluating. A routine that uses a fixed 10 terms is accurate for late-time settlement and wrong for early-time settlement, and it will never tell you which case you are in. Always stop on a measured criterion, never on a fixed count.

3. Building a series summation properly

import numpy as np

def consolidation_U(T, es=1e-8, max_terms=2000):
    """Average degree of consolidation by Terzaghi's series.

    T         : time factor, dimensionless
    es        : stopping tolerance on the approximate relative error, percent
    Returns   : (U, terms_used, ea)
    """
    if T <= 0:
        return 0.0, 0, 0.0

    total = 0.0
    ea = float('inf')
    for m in range(max_terms):
        M = np.pi * (2*m + 1) / 2
        term = (2 / M**2) * np.exp(-M**2 * T)
        old = total
        total += term
        if total > 0:
            ea = abs((total - old) / total) * 100
        if ea < es:
            return 1 - total, m + 1, ea
    return 1 - total, max_terms, ea

Three features of this routine are the point of the lab, and all three are commonly omitted:

  1. It stops on a measured error, not a term count. The criterion is the approximate percent relative error εa — the change the last term made, relative to the running total.
  2. It caps the loop. A series that fails to converge must terminate anyway, with the number of terms used reported so the caller can detect it.
  3. It reports how hard it worked. Returning terms_used and ea turns an opaque number into a result with provenance.

How many figures is εs worth?

The Scarborough criterion converts a demand for significant figures into a tolerance:

εs = (0.5 × 102−n) %   →   3 figures: 0.05 %,   6 figures: 5×10−5 %

Settlement predictions are rarely worth more than three significant figures, because the coefficient of consolidation is seldom known to better than a factor of two. Summing to 10−12 is arithmetic theatre. Match the tolerance to the data, and say so in the report.

Work through the first term by hand to confirm the mechanics: M = π/2 = 1.570796, so M2 = 2.467401. Then 2/M2 = 0.810569 and e−2.467401(0.2) = e−0.493480 = 0.610500. Their product is 0.494851.

What the table shows

U = 0.5041, which is the textbook value at T = 0.2.

The second term contributes 0.2 % and the third contributes 0.00003 %. Each term is smaller than the previous one by roughly a factor of 500, because the exponent grows as m2. Two terms give three significant figures; three terms give six. A routine that always sums ten terms is doing seven terms of useless arithmetic here — and, at T = 0.001, is stopping eighteen terms too early.

The stopping decision is therefore made by εa, not by the programmer. With εs = 0.05 % (three significant figures, by the Scarborough criterion) the loop stops after two terms. With εs = 5×10−5 % it stops after three.

5. Series that behave badly

5.1 Terms that grow before they shrink

The exponential series ∑xk/k! converges for every x. For x = −20 it converges to 2.06×10−9 — but the largest term along the way is about 4.3×107. The computer must add and subtract numbers of order 107 and land on 10−9. The leading sixteen digits cancel and what remains is round-off.

def exp_series(x, n_terms=200):
    term, total, largest = 1.0, 1.0, 1.0
    for k in range(1, n_terms):
        term *= x / k
        total += term
        largest = max(largest, abs(term))
    return total, largest

approx, largest = exp_series(-20.0)
print(approx, np.exp(-20.0), largest)
# the approximation is wrong by orders of magnitude, and may even be negative
The diagnostic, and the cure

Diagnostic: compare the largest term encountered with the final answer. If the ratio is 10k, you have lost about k significant figures to cancellation.

Cure: reformulate so that nothing cancels. Here, sum the series for e+20, where every term is positive, and take the reciprocal. Algebraically identical, numerically far better. Whenever a formula subtracts two nearly equal quantities, look for an equivalent that does not.

5.2 Series that converge too slowly to be useful

The harmonic-like alternating series ∑(−1)k+1/k converges to ln 2, but the error after n terms is about 1/(2n). Three correct decimal places require about 500 terms; six require half a million. A series can be perfectly convergent and still be the wrong way to compute the number.

5.3 The floor imposed by the machine

def machine_epsilon():
    """Smallest eps with 1.0 + eps != 1.0."""
    eps = 1.0
    while 1.0 + eps/2.0 != 1.0:
        eps /= 2.0
    return eps

print(machine_epsilon())      # 2.220446049250313e-16

About 2.2×10−16, or roughly sixteen significant decimal digits. Once a term is smaller than εmach times the running total, adding it changes nothing at all. Setting a tolerance below that produces an infinite loop, not a better answer. This is the ceiling on every calculation in this course.

6. Estimating the error you did not compute

For an alternating series with decreasing terms, the truncation error is bounded by the first term omitted — a rare case where the error bound is free. For a positive series such as the consolidation sum, no such bound exists in general, so use one of these:

  1. Compare with a tighter tolerance. Sum to 10−6, then to 10−10, and take the difference as the error estimate of the first.
  2. Bound the tail analytically. For the consolidation series the omitted terms are dominated by a geometric decay, so the tail is bounded by a multiple of the last term computed.
  3. Check against an independent formula. The standard approximations T = πU2/4 for U < 0.6 and T = 1.781 − 0.933 log10(100 − U%) above it agree with the series to about 1 %. Disagreement signals an error in your summation, not in the textbook.

7. Where it breaks

Fixed term count

Accurate at one operating point, silently wrong at another. The most common defect in inherited engineering code.

Tolerance below machine epsilon

The loop cannot satisfy it and runs to the cap. Always cap, and always report whether the cap was reached.

Cancellation

Large intermediate terms, small final answer. Detect by tracking the largest term; cure by reformulating.

Evaluating at the boundary

At T = 0 the series sums to 1 and U = 0, but convergence there is infinitely slow. Handle boundary values as special cases rather than letting the loop grind.
