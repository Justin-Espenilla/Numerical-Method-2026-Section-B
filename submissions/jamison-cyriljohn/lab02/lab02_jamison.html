Second derivative, read: The fitted second derivative is a clean, two-lobed signal hidden entirely inside logger-rounding noise in the raw second difference (compare the scatter to the smooth orange curve below): positive while the inflow is still accelerating the rise, crossing to negative once the rise decelerates into the crest, staying negative through the recession, then returning toward zero as the level settles onto its new plateau - i.e. the inflow pulse itself rose, peaked, and fell away, it did not simply switch on and stay on.

Model, fit, and residuals

Model chosen: A single logistic or Gompertz assumes one monotonic rise settling onto a ceiling; this record rises, overshoots to a crest, and then recedes to a new plateau, so a monotonic model cannot fit it. The sum-of-two-logistics form supplies a rising limb (a1, k1, t01) for the flood pulse and a second, slower, negative-amplitude limb (a2, k2, t02) for the recession, letting the curve overshoot and settle - the same 'two pulses' structure the activity sheet suggests, used here for one pulse's rise and its own recession rather than two separate rainfall bands.

Residual plots

Reading the residuals: The residual mean drifts across the record (thirds: +0.007, -0.006, -0.001 m) rather than sitting flat on zero, and there are only 16 sign changes over 287 possible steps -- long same-sign runs during the rise and again during the recession. That is a shape problem (a slower pre-event onset than a pure logistic pair can reproduce), not sensor noise. The spread does not simply widen with level (corr(|e|, fitted h) = -0.32); it is largest where the curvature is sharpest, around the crest and the recession knee. The largest single residual is 0.221 m, about 22x the logger's own 1 cm resolution -- small next to the 6-7 m rise, but not explained by rounding alone. With R2 = 0.9991 sitting on top of patterned residuals, this is an honest partial fit: the two-logistic shape captures the pulse well but not perfectly.

Units: h is in meters and t is in hours, so A is in meter·hours - a level integrated over time, not a volume (that would need the reservoir's stage-to-area or stage-to-volume curve as well).

Why quad and trapezoid disagree: quad integrates the continuous fitted curve; trapezoid sums straight line segments between the logger's own centimeter-rounded points. The -0.025 m·h gap is small (0.00% of the trapezoid total) and comes from exactly that: the fit smooths through the rounding and through the residual pattern noted above, while the trapezoid rule takes the raw points, kinks and all, literally.

What this means to the flood-control office: A is a compact single number for how high, and for how long, the reservoir stood above the zero datum over this record - useful for comparing this event to others on the same gauge. It says nothing by itself about discharge, inflow volume, or downstream risk; those need the stage-discharge (rating) relationship for this specific reservoir, which is not part of this dataset.
